A summer has already gone by, and another just turning the corner since my trip
to China in 2019.  I wanted to talk about a special conversation I had with
my grandfather, whom I had only seen a handful of times prior to this trip, and
whom I had never really gotten the chance to have a real talk with.<!--break-->

It was a sweltering summer day in Changchun, when we got off the bus at 
Changyang district, where we met up with my grandmother (my dad's stepmother,
whom my parents addressed as Wang Yi [Aunt Wang]).
She looked the same as I had remembered, except that she had aged considerably,
her black hair had grayed, and it was clear that making the walk to meet us here
in the heat had taken a toll on her stamina.  Still, I appreciated that she
came to greet us right at the stop, and together with my mother and younger 
brother, we walked together to the little street where there apartment was.

The walk was not long, and though we were not a far way off from center city,
it felt as if we had walked right up to the end of town.  The road was unpaved,
and the surroundings looked like desert.  The street was lined on one side by
a row of decrepit old four or five story buildings that had shopfronts at the
ground level.  Once, these buildings were the domiciles of the faculty of the
nearby Jilin University.

We approached a door situated between two shops, so concealed that I would have
not noticed it had we not stopped in front of it, and from here we entered into
a stairwell and ascended two flights of stairs where there were two doors.
The right door had a friendly sign posted on the door, a red poster with "Fu"
(luck) on it, like many residential doors in China.  The other was less 
welcoming, austere with nothing more than a brown mat at the doorstep.  My
brother and I exchanged looks; having heard many stories of what our grandfather
was like we had a feeling we knew which door it was going to be.  Our
suspicions were confirmed when Wang Yi opened the plain door to the left.

There he was, a slight man, now with a hunch that made him even shorter than
he otherwise would have been, a few whisps of long silvery white hair combed
over the dome of his square head, and squinty little eyes that peered at us
with intense curiosity and emotion.  Once he would always appear with a pair
of glasses, though age had rendered them unnecessary.  In his right ear there
was a hearing aid; the other had already gone deaf.  Immediately, he took my
hand and said, "Lu sen", my brother's name.  Wang Yi was about to correct him
when he vigorously shook his head and tried again.  He said, "Lu nan", my name 
and I nodded my head.  I don't recall any time ever my grandfather had addressed
me directly, as it was always an indirect interaction.  My parents would 
instruct me to give my regards to my grandfather (yeye hao), and I'd be off on
my way.  Perhaps this was the first and perhaps the only time he would get to 
talk to me in person.

We sat together on in the common room to our right, my grandfather, brother, and
I on the couch, Wang Yi on a chair to our left and my mother on the chair to
our right, and immediately, my grandfather began huffing, and tears began
streaming from his eyes.  "These," he said, "these are my grandchildren!" and
he placed his head on my brother's lap.  The moment was intense and even my 
mom, who often voiced her disapproval of my grandfather, had to fight back
her moistening eyes.

When my grandfather had calmed down and began to breathe normally again, he
detailed to us his ills as an old man.  Advanced age, he said, brings out
surprising youth in people.  He pointed to the bristles on his face and
explained how he had never been able to grow much facial hair, and yet here
it was.  Then pointed to his neck and said he was afflicted with
an illness that typically was only found in small children (it may have 
been mumps).  He complained of dry eyes and his waning sight and explained
that he was completely deaf when he removed the hearing aid in his right ear.
Despite all this, though, it was clear that while his senses were dulled,
his mind was quite lucid, and his speech easily comprehendable.

We browsed through old photos, which we breezed through.  We saw a photo of
our grandmother, who passed away when my father was twelve, and we saw photos
of my grandfather as a young man in Vladivostok, Russia.  I wish I had more time
to fully soak in the photos, but it seemed my grandfather had little interest
in them, and our little visual trip down memory lane over over within a minute.

As I had just graduated from college, our initial conversation was about life,
and I got to hear from my grandfather himself his story.  I talked a bit about
college, some of accolades I received (which my mother insisted I share),
and about the industry I was going in (computers).  My grandfather approved,
but continued to add some of his own advice on top, after all, as an old man
he cannot help but to 'jiaoxun' (teach) us.

He related to us a bit of history, how his father had come from Shandong to
the port city of Dandong, how he grew up there before going to school in
Changchun, Jilin, where he resides today.  He explained how difficult it was
to get a decent education in his time.  As a young kid growing up during
Japanese occupation, primary school instruction was in Japanese, and the 
kids spared no expense to skip classes and pull pranks on their teachers.
During the civil war, schools were closed due to the fighting.  And when
things had finally calmed down, he was nearly a grown man, and desperately
tried to complete a high school education.  His father would reprimand him
for trying so hard to get educated.  "Studying what books?  You're studying to
become a squirrel!" (du shenme shu?  du ge laoshu!) he'd often say.  A textile
merchant, my great grandfather believed school education made one naive in the 
practical realities of life, and that the best kind of education was an 
apprenticeship in a trade.  He sent my grandfather to a bone doctor to
apprentice in traditional medicine, which my grandfather disliked, and he
would often escape the apprenticeship so he could take classes at the local
high school.

For someone with little primary school education, classes were challenging. 
He could barely read, and literary analysis was beyond him.  However, he found
a kindred spirit in science and mathematics, and this became the path he wished
to follow after he had completed his secondary education.
